Lesson 1: What Is Reinforcement Learning?

Start here. You need no math and no prior machine-learning knowledge. By the end of this lesson you will know what reinforcement learning is, the words people use to describe it, and how it differs from other kinds of machine learning.

After this lesson you can:
  • Explain reinforcement learning (RL) in one sentence, using an everyday example.
  • Name the parts of an RL problem: agent, environment, state, action, reward, policy and value.
  • Describe the agent–environment loop and write down a trajectory.
  • Say how RL differs from supervised and unsupervised learning.
Contents

1. Learning by trial and error

Think about how a child learns to ride a bicycle. Nobody gives the child a list of the right muscle movements for every moment. The child tries, wobbles, falls, tries again, and slowly figures out what keeps the bike upright. The world gives feedback: falling hurts, riding smoothly feels great. Over many attempts, the child's behaviour improves.

Reinforcement learning is the branch of machine learning that copies this idea. A computer program, called the agent, learns how to behave by interacting with a world, called the environment. Nobody tells the agent the correct action. Instead, after each action it receives a number, called a reward, that says how good the outcome was. The agent's job is to discover, by trial and error, which actions lead to the most reward in the long run.

Analogy: training a puppy You say "sit". The puppy does something random: it jumps, barks, lies down, or sits. When it happens to sit, it gets a treat. After enough repetitions, the puppy learns that sitting when it hears "sit" earns treats. The puppy is the agent, you and the room are the environment, the treat is the reward, and "what to do when I hear a word" is the policy the puppy learns.

Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto, who wrote the standard textbook on the subject, describe RL as learning what to do (how to map situations to actions) so as to maximize a numerical reward signal. Two features make RL special:

  • Trial and error: the agent is not told which action is best. It must try actions and see what happens.
  • Delayed reward: an action can affect not only the next reward, but also every situation and reward after it. A good move now may only pay off much later.

2. The agent–environment loop

Every RL problem has the same basic shape: a loop between the agent and the environment. Watch the animation: the dots carry information around the loop.

Animation

At each time step t = 0, 1, 2, …

  1. The agent observes the current state Sₜ (what the situation looks like now).
  2. Using its policy, the agent chooses an action Aₜ.
  3. The environment reacts. It moves to a new state Sₜ₊₁ and gives a reward Rₜ₊₁ (a single number).
  4. The agent uses this experience to learn: it adjusts its policy so that good outcomes become more likely. Then the loop repeats.

Writing down everything that happens gives a sequence called a trajectory:

S₀, A₀, R₁, S₁, A₁, R₂, S₂, A₂, R₃, …

Why is the reward numbered t+1? Because it arrives after the action taken at time t, together with the next state. This is the convention used in the Sutton and Barto textbook.

3. Try it: you are the agent

Below is a small world of 4×4 squares. You control the robot. You do not know where the good and bad squares are: squares are hidden until you step on them, which is exactly the situation a real agent is in at the start of learning.

  • Every move costs −1 (so a shorter route is better).
  • Somewhere there is a goal worth +10 and a trap worth −10. Both end the episode.
  • Walking into the outer wall leaves you where you are (and still costs −1).
Interactive game

Play a few episodes. In the first one you have to explore. Once you know the map, you can go straight to the goal. That change, from exploring to using what you learned, is what RL algorithms do automatically.

What did you just do? You played the role of an RL agent:

  • The state was your square (row, column).
  • Your actions were Up, Down, Left, Right.
  • The rewards were −1, +10 or −10.
  • The episode was one game, from start until you reached the goal or the trap.
  • The return was your total reward for the episode. The best possible return here is +5: the shortest safe route takes six moves, five of them cost −1 and the last one lands on the goal and pays +10.
  • After a few episodes, you had a policy: a rule in your head like "from here, go up".

4. The vocabulary of RL

These seven words appear in every lesson that follows. Take time to understand each one.

AgentThe learner and decision maker. It observes, acts, and learns.The robot (you)
EnvironmentEverything outside the agent. It responds to actions with new states and rewards. The agent cannot change its rules.The 4×4 world and its hidden squares
State (s)All the information that is useful for choosing the next action.Your current square
Action (a)One of the choices the agent can make. The set of all actions is the action space.Up, Down, Left, Right
Reward (r)A single number (a scalar) the environment sends after each action. It measures how good that step was. It can be positive, zero or negative.−1 per move, +10 goal, −10 trap
Policy (π)The agent's strategy: a rule that maps each state to an action (or to probabilities of actions). Learning a good policy is the goal."In the bottom-left corner, go right"

Reward vs. value Reward is like the pleasure or pain you feel right now. Value is like your judgment of how good your whole situation is. Paying for university gives a negative reward today but puts you in a high-value state. RL agents choose actions based on value, not only on immediate reward.

5. The goal: the most reward in the long run

RL is built on the reward hypothesis: every goal we care about can be described as maximizing the expected cumulative reward, meaning the total reward collected over time, on average.

The word cumulative is important. An agent that only grabs the biggest reward right now is usually not the best agent. In chess, sacrificing your queen loses material now but can win the game. In the grid game, a move that costs −1 is worth it if it brings you closer to +10.

The credit assignment problem When a chess game is won after 40 moves, which moves deserved the credit? The reward arrives only at the end, but the decisions were made much earlier. Spreading credit back to the earlier actions that caused a reward is one of the central difficulties of RL, and much of Day 2 is about how algorithms solve it.

Episodes and continuing tasks

  • Episodic tasks end naturally: a game finishes, a robot reaches its goal. Each run from start to end is an episode, and then everything resets.
  • Continuing tasks go on without end: a traffic light runs day and night. Lesson 2 shows how a discount factor keeps the total reward finite in this case.

6. How RL differs from other machine learning

Machine learning is often divided into three families. Compare what each one receives and what it tries to do.

Diagram
Supervised learningUnsupervised learningReinforcement learning
DataInputs with correct answers (labels)Inputs onlyExperience the agent creates by acting
Feedback"The right answer was X"None"That was worth +3", but not what would have been best
GoalPredict the label of new inputsFind groups or patternsMaximize total reward over time
Decisions affect future data?NoNoYes: the agent's actions decide what it sees next
Common mistake "Rewards are just labels." They are not. A label says which answer is correct. A reward only says how good the chosen action turned out. The agent never learns what would have happened with the other actions unless it tries them. This is why RL needs exploration (Lesson 3).

7. The two main families of RL methods

An RL agent must answer one question over and over: how do I choose a good action? There are two main approaches, and this course covers both.

Where is RL used in practice? Programs that learned to play Go, chess and video games better than humans, robots that learn to walk or grasp objects, traffic-light controllers, and systems that choose which advertisement or news article to show (a version of the multi-armed bandit problem in Lesson 7).

8. Summary

  • RL is learning what to do by trial and error, guided by a reward signal.
  • The agent observes a state, takes an action, and receives a reward and a new state. This loop repeats.
  • The agent's strategy is its policy. Its goal is to maximize the cumulative reward, not just the next reward.
  • Unlike supervised learning, RL is never told the correct action, and its own choices decide what data it sees.
